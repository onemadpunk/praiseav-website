    Screens and livestream

    Live captions

    Live captions turn what's said into the microphone into words along the bottom of the congregation screen: the sermon, the notices, the prayers. They help people who are hard of hearing, and anyone sitting where the sound is poor.

    The speech is recognised on this computer. Nothing that's said is sent anywhere or kept.

    Turning them on

    1. In the right-hand column, open Live captions.
    2. Choose the Microphone (see below).
    3. Tick On.

    The first time, PraiseAV downloads its speech recognition from GitHub: about 100 MB, once, so it needs the internet that one time. (If the church’s web filter blocks it, PraiseAV says so: ask for github.com to be allowed, or turn captions on at home first.) A bar shows how far it's got. After that, captions work without the internet.

    When it says Listening, speak and the words appear, on the screen and in the panel. They show while someone is still talking and are tidied up when they pause. They clear after a few seconds of quiet.

    The best sound

    Captions are only as good as the sound they hear.

    • Best: a cable from the sound desk (a spare aux or record output) into the computer: into its microphone socket, or a USB sound adaptor, which costs a few pounds. The words come out clean, without the room's echo and the congregation's coughs.
    • All right: a USB microphone near the preacher, or on the lectern.
    • Not good: the laptop's own microphone at the back of the room. It hears the whole room.

    Songs

    While a song is on the screen, captions pause: the song's words are already there, and singing doesn't caption well. To caption during songs too (say, for a solo whose words aren't on screen), tick Show during songs too.

    Size and place

    Choose Small, Normal or Large. The captions sit in a dark strip along the bottom of the congregation screen, above any parent alerts, and the slide's words move up to make room.

    What to expect

    Like any speech recognition, it sometimes mishears: names, Bible places and unusual words most of all, and when two people talk at once. It's good for following along, but it isn't a transcript.

    It works best on a computer bought in the last five years or so. On an older one the words may come a second or two late.

    Credits

    The speech recognition is NVIDIA's NeMo streaming FastConformer English model (licensed CC BY 4.0), run with the free sherpa-onnx toolkit from the k2-fsa project (Apache 2.0).
